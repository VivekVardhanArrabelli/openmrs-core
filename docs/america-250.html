The Forty Extra Years

America turns 250 today. Set the parades aside for a moment and look at the republic's quietest achievement: an American born in 1776 could expect about 38 years of life. A child born this morning can expect nearly 79.

The evidence

Two and a half centuries, one line

For America's first 125 years the line barely moved — a newborn in 1776 couldn't expect to see 40, and a century later still couldn't. Then sanitation, vaccines, antisepsis, antibiotics, and public health bent the line upward, steeply, in a single century. Hover the line; the gold dots are the moments that bent it.

The experiment continues

The next point on the line
gets written in places like this

This page was built, on the nation's 250th birthday, inside the source code of OpenMRS — the open-source medical record system that grew out of Indianapolis and Boston and now runs in clinics and hospitals across dozens of countries. It charts patients, not fireworks: every visit, every vaccination, every vital sign, recorded so the next clinician can see the line and bend it a little further.

That may be the most American thing on this page. A nation founded on a written claim — that the point of government is life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness — keeps finding new ways to write the life part down and give the writing away. Washington's inoculation order, the one-room lab that became the NIH, Salk refusing to patent the polio vaccine (“Could you patent the sun?”), open-source medicine — one long argument that the extra years belong to everyone.
